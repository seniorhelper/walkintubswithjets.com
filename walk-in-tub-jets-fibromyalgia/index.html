Jetted walk-in tub for fibromyalgia: what the research shows

Direct answer: warm-water treatment shows a small, short-term pain benefit for fibromyalgia in a 2014 review of randomized trials (standardized mean difference −0.42 for hydrotherapy), but nobody has tested home walk-in tubs or their jets for it. Treat a jetted walk-in tub as a comfort purchase, not a treatment. The review found no clear effect on depression, and its authors warned that the benefit may be overestimated. Ask your doctor first.

Reviewed November 2026. This page is general information, not medical advice.

What the 2014 review found

The most-cited summary we found is a systematic review and meta-analysis by Johannes Naumann and Catharina Sadaghiani in Arthritis Research & Therapy (2014). It pooled randomized controlled trials of two things: hydrotherapy (warm-water treatment programs) and balneotherapy (treatment in mineral or thermal spa water). The review included 24 trials in its write-up and 21 in the statistics.

OutcomeHydrotherapySpa (balneotherapy)
Pain, end of treatmentSmall drop (−0.42; 8 trials, 462 people)Larger drop (−0.84), but the trials disagreed with each other
Daily function and quality of lifeSmall gain (−0.40)Larger gain (−0.78)
DepressionNo significant effectNo significant effect
Tender point countNo significant effectLower
Pain at follow-upSmaller (−0.25)Smaller (−0.30)

Numbers are standardized mean differences, where a minus sign means less pain or disability and about 0.2 is a small effect and 0.5 a medium one. Source: Naumann and Sadaghiani 2014, listed below.

In plain terms: on average, people who took part in a warm-water treatment program had somewhat less pain than people who did not, right after the program. Part of that benefit was still visible at follow-up, but smaller.

What the studies could not show

  • They were not about walk-in tubs. The trials tested treatment programs, not jets in a home walk-in tub, and we found no trial of home walk-in tubs for fibromyalgia.
  • The evidence is thin. The authors describe the studies as small and often at unclear or high risk of bias; only 5 of 24 were rated low risk. Blinding was not possible, because people know whether they are in warm water.
  • Mostly women. More than 96% of participants were women, and long-term data were limited.
  • The authors urge caution. They wrote that there is a risk of overestimating how well hydrotherapy, and even more so spa treatment, works.

Our broader hydrotherapy research summary covers the evidence on warm baths and jets for other conditions, and the arthritis page explains safe water temperature in detail.

What this means when choosing a tub

If you are considering a walk-in tub because fibromyalgia makes stepping over a tub wall hard, the case for a tub rests on entry and comfort, not on jets as therapy. A few practical points:

  • Warmth first. Warm water is the part the trials had in common. A tub that holds temperature while you soak, such as one with an inline heater or heated seat, matters more than jet count.
  • Fill and drain waiting. You sit through both. Check the fill and drain time for a tub you are considering, since a long wait can be uncomfortable.
  • Gentle options. Air jets are gentler than water jets, and any jet should be adjustable or switchable off. See the air vs water jet chooser.
  • Running cost. Jets and heaters use electricity and hot water. The operating cost calculator shows what to expect.
  • Know the cautions. Read who should not use a jetted tub before you buy.

No bathing product removes all risk of falls or injury. Talk with your doctor or an occupational therapist about what fits your health and your bathroom.

Questions people ask

Can a walk-in tub with jets help fibromyalgia pain?

Possibly a little, but nobody has tested home walk-in tubs. A 2014 review of randomized trials found that supervised warm-water treatment lowered fibromyalgia pain by a small amount at the end of treatment, with a smaller effect at follow-up. The trials were not done in home walk-in tubs, and the authors warn the benefit may be overestimated.

Does hydrotherapy help fibromyalgia depression or tender points?

In that review, hydrotherapy did not show a statistically significant effect on depression or on the tender point count. Mineral and thermal spa treatment showed a larger drop in tender points, but those trials varied a lot and were small.

Should I use water jets or air jets with fibromyalgia?

No study compares them for fibromyalgia. Many people start with gentle air jets or the warm water alone and add water jets on low. Our air vs water jet chooser explains the difference. If pressure on tender areas hurts, turn the jets down or off.

Should I talk to my doctor before buying a jetted tub?

Yes. Fibromyalgia often comes with other conditions, and heat, jets and getting in and out of a tub affect different people differently. Ask your doctor or a physical or occupational therapist, and read who should not use a jetted tub first.
