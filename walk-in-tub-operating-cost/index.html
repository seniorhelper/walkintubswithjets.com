What does a jetted walk-in tub cost to run? Work it out with your own numbers

Short answer: the jets themselves are cheap to run. A 1,000-watt pump for 15 minutes uses a quarter of a kilowatt-hour, which is under a nickel at the U.S. average rate. The bigger cost is the hot water you fill the tub with: warming 50 gallons from 55°F to 100°F takes roughly 6 kWh with an electric water heater, or a little over a dollar a bath at 18.8¢ per kWh. The calculator below uses your rate and your tub’s watts.

The formula, in plain words

Electricity is sold by the kilowatt-hour (kWh). One kWh is 1,000 watts running for one hour. So for each motor:

kWh = watts × minutes ÷ 60 ÷ 1,000 cost = kWh × your rate in dollars

For hot water, it takes 8.34 BTU to warm one gallon of water by one degree Fahrenheit (a gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds). One kWh equals 3,412 BTU, and one therm of gas equals 100,000 BTU. The water heater wastes some heat, so we divide by its efficiency:

BTU = gallons × 8.34 × (bath °F − cold °F) electric kWh = BTU ÷ 3,412 ÷ efficiency gas therms = BTU ÷ 100,000 ÷ efficiency

A worked example

Take the Escape figures above with 50 gallons, 55°F incoming water, a 100°F bath, an electric tank heater at 0.92 efficiency and 18.83¢/kWh:

PartMathPer bath
Water jets1,000 W × 15 min = 0.25 kWhabout 5¢
Air jets500 W × 15 min = 0.125 kWhabout 2¢
Inline heater650 W × 15 min = 0.16 kWhabout 3¢
Hot water50 × 8.34 × 45 = 18,765 BTU ÷ 3,412 ÷ 0.92 = 5.98 kWhabout $1.13
Totalabout 6.5 kWhabout $1.23

The motors are under 10% of the bill in this example. That surprises most people, and it changes where savings come from.

Where the savings really are

  • Fill only as high as you need. Every 10 gallons you skip saves about a fifth of the hot-water cost in the example. With water jets, you still need the level above the top jet.
  • Take a slightly cooler bath. Dropping from 102°F to 98°F cuts the temperature rise, and the hot-water cost, by close to 9% when the incoming water is 55°F.
  • Keep the water heater at a safe setting. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission has long recommended 120°F at the water heater to prevent scalds, and older adults are among those most at risk. Hotter storage does not make a bath cheaper; it just raises scald risk and standby loss.
  • Do not worry much about jet minutes. Running the pump 10 extra minutes costs about 3¢ at the default rate in the example.
  • Compare to a hot tub fairly. A backyard spa keeps its water hot around the clock; heating sites put typical spa electricity at about $30–$100 a month. A walk-in tub is filled and drained each bath, so it has no standby heating, but it pays to heat fresh water every time.

Make sure your water heater can keep up. Cost is one question; having enough hot water to fill the tub is another. A guide on our sister site explains how to tell if your water heater is big enough. Remember that an inline heater holds temperature during the bath; it does not heat cold water.

How to find your tub’s watts

  1. The spec sheet. Search the model name plus “spec sheet.” Look for pump, blower (air) and heater lines.
  2. Amps instead of watts? Multiply amps by volts. A 5-amp pump on 120 volts is about 600 watts.
  3. Horsepower? One horsepower is about 746 watts, so a 0.5 HP motor is roughly 373 watts of output (it draws a bit more).
  4. The label on the motor. Your installer can read it from the access panel.

Questions people ask

Is a jetted walk-in tub expensive to run?

Usually not. In our worked example, one bath costs about $1.23, and over 90% of that is heating the fill water. Your cost depends mostly on gallons, water temperatures and your energy prices. Use the calculator with your own numbers.

Does the inline heater use a lot of power?

A typical inline heater on a walk-in tub is rated in the hundreds of watts; Ella’s Bubbles lists 650 W on several models. Fifteen minutes at 650 W is about 0.16 kWh, roughly 3¢ at the U.S. average rate.

How do I find my electricity rate?

Divide the total electric charges on your bill by the kWh you used that month. That “all-in” rate includes delivery fees and is the most honest number to use.

Why does the calculator ask about incoming water temperature?

Your water heater has to warm the cold supply up to bath temperature. Water arriving at 45°F in a cold climate needs more energy than water arriving at 70°F. That single number can swing your hot-water cost by a third.

Will a heat pump water heater lower the cost?

Heat pump water heaters move heat rather than make it, so their efficiency rating is well above 1. Choose that type in the calculator and type the UEF from your heater’s spec sheet to see the effect.
