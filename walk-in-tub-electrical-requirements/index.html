Wiring and safety

Walk-in tub electrical requirements, explained for homeowners

Short answer: most jetted walk-in tubs run on ordinary 120-volt power, but they need their own GFCI-protected circuit, and many need one circuit for each motor or heater. A typical manual asks for one or more dedicated 15- or 20-amp GFCI circuits run straight from the panel. The installation manual for your exact model is the final word, and a licensed electrician should do the work.

Reviewed October 2026. This page explains requirements in plain words; it is not a wiring guide.

Why jetted tubs have special wiring rules

Water, people and motors share a small space in a bathroom. The National Electrical Code has a separate part for hydromassage bathtubs (Article 680, Part VII). Trade explanations of section 680.71 describe two key rules:

  • An individual branch circuit. The tub’s motor and its equipment get their own circuit from the panel, not one shared with the bathroom lights or a hair dryer.
  • A readily accessible GFCI. A ground-fault circuit interrupter cuts power in a fraction of a second if current leaks where it should not. “Readily accessible” means you can reach it to test or reset it without moving the tub or opening a wall.

Two more points from the same part of the code are worth knowing: ordinary 125-volt receptacles within 6 feet of the tub’s inside walls also need GFCI protection, and the tub’s electrical equipment has to stay reachable without damaging the building finish. That is why walk-in tubs have removable access panels.

Cities and counties adopt different editions of the code and sometimes add their own rules. Your electrician and local permit office decide what applies to your home.

What manufacturer manuals ask for

Requirements differ by model, which is why the question “how many amps does a walk-in tub need?” has no single answer. Here is what published documents say:

A Hydro Systems install guideDedicated 20-amp GFCI for the pump, 15-amp for the blower and 15-amp for the heater

The pattern: plan on at least one new dedicated GFCI circuit, and expect two or three for a combination tub with a heater. An air-only tub with no heater is usually the simplest.

Testing the GFCI at home

Manuals for jetted tubs ask owners to test GFCI protection regularly. Press the TEST button; the jets should stop and the device should trip. Press RESET to restore power. If it does not trip, do not use the jets until an electrician checks it. Once a month is an easy habit to remember.

Questions people ask

Can a walk-in tub plug into a regular outlet?

Some models come with a plug, but manuals still require that outlet to be on its own GFCI-protected circuit run directly from the panel. A shared bathroom outlet is not the same thing.

Does a walk-in tub need 240 volts?

Most residential walk-in tubs we have seen use 120-volt equipment. Some larger heaters or special models differ. The spec sheet lists voltage for each component.

Why does my tub need more than one circuit?

Each motor and the heater draw current at the same time. If the total is more than one circuit can carry, or the maker simply requires separation, each gets its own breaker. Try the amp checker above with your numbers.

Is the GFCI the same as a regular breaker?

No. A regular breaker protects the wires from overload. A GFCI protects people by cutting power when it senses current leaking, for example through water. Jetted tubs need GFCI protection.

Can I do the wiring myself?

Manuals say a licensed electrician should do it, and many towns require a permit. Incorrect wiring near water is dangerous and can void the warranty.
