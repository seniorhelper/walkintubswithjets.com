Hydrotherapy jets in walk-in tubs

Walk-in tubs with jets, explained with real numbers

Air jets, water jets, combination and microbubble systems: what each one does, what it takes to wire and run, who should skip them, and what the research actually shows. Free tools, published by Aging Safely Baths.

Direct answer: a walk-in tub with jets is a door-entry tub with water jets (a pump, firm aimed massage), air jets (a blower, gentle bubbles all over) or both. Most run on 120 volts but need their own GFCI-protected circuits under NEC 680.71, often two or three. The jets cost about 10¢ a bath to run; heating the fill water costs about $1.31 at the U.S. average electric rate. Water jets need a monthly cleaning flush. The warmth has better research behind it than the jets do, so choose jets for comfort and check with a doctor if you have heart or blood pressure problems.

Every model lists dedicated GFCI-protected 110/120 V lines, an ozone unit on the water side, and an air system that dries its lines after use. Microbubble, where offered, adds a 1 HP, 7.3 A pump.

Three things jump out. Water-jet tubs carry 14 to 26 nozzles, not the hundreds some ads suggest. A two-seat tub has two water pumps, so it needs more circuits. And the inline heater draws as much as a pump; it keeps warm water warm but cannot heat a cold fill.

Air, water or both?

Water jets

A pump recirculates bath water through adjustable nozzles aimed at the back, hips, calves and feet. Firm and targeted. Needs plain water and a monthly flush.

Air jets

A blower pushes warmed air through small jets in the seat and floor. Gentle all over and kind to fragile skin. The lines purge themselves.

Combination

Both systems with separate controls. Best when two people share the tub or you want both feelings. More circuits, more cleaning.

What jets can and cannot do for your health

Warm water eases stiffness, and a Cochrane review found warm-water exercise brings small short-term gains for knee and hip arthritis. A warm bath an hour or two before bed helps many people fall asleep sooner. But we found no controlled trials of jets in a home tub, so treat them as comfort, not treatment. Our research summary rates each claim.

Check with a doctor first if you have heart disease, blood pressure that drops when you stand, diabetes with numb feet, open wounds, or take medicines that make you drowsy. Keep the water at 92 to 100°F and stand up slowly. See who should not use jets for the sources.

Questions people ask

What is a walk-in tub with jets?

A bathtub with a watertight door in the side, so you step in over a low threshold, sit down and then fill it. Jets add massage: water jets use a pump to push bath water through nozzles, and air jets use a blower to push warm air through small holes.

What is the difference between air jets and water jets?

Water jets are fewer, stronger and aimable, good for one sore area, and need a monthly cleaning flush. Air jets are gentle bubbles all over, kinder to fragile skin and easier to keep clean. Combination tubs have both.

How many jets does a walk-in tub have?

On Ella’s Bubbles spec sheets, water-jet systems have 14 to 26 nozzles depending on the model, and air systems 14 to 18 jets. More is not automatically better; placement at your back, calves and feet matters more.

Does a walk-in tub with jets need special wiring?

Yes. NEC 680.71 requires an individual branch circuit with a readily accessible GFCI, and many tubs need one circuit per pump, blower or heater. An Ella Deluxe draws about 18 amps with everything on.

How much does it cost to run the jets?

About 10 cents a bath for a 1,000-watt pump, 500-watt blower and 650-watt heater running 15 minutes each, at the July 2026 U.S. average of 18.31 cents per kWh. Heating a 60-gallon fill with an electric water heater costs about $1.31 more.

Can jets be added to a walk-in tub later?

In practice, no. Pumps, blowers and jet plumbing are built into the shell at the factory, so decide before you order.

Are jetted tubs safe for seniors?

For most, yes, with warm rather than hot water, a short first soak and a slow stand at the end. People with heart disease, blood pressure that drops on standing, numb feet or drowsy medicines should check with a doctor first.

Can I open the door while the tub is full?

No. The water holds the door against its seal, and opening it would flood the floor. You stay seated while the tub drains, so a fast drain and a warm towel help.
