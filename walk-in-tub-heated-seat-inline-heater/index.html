Heated seat, heated backrest and inline heater: how a walk-in tub keeps you warm

Direct answer: a walk-in tub can have two kinds of heat. A heated seat and backrest warms what you sit against; Ella’s runs on a 30-minute timer, switched on 5 to 10 minutes early. An inline heater holds the water’s temperature while the jets run, but cannot reheat a cooled bath. Neither replaces a warm bathroom.

Why do people get cold in a walk-in tub?

A walk-in tub changes the order of a bath. In a regular tub you fill first and then climb in. In a walk-in tub you step through the door, close it, sit down and then fill, because the water would pour out of an open door. At the end the order flips again: the water holds the door against its seal, so you stay seated until the tub has drained. That gives every bath two stretches where you sit with little or no warm water around you: the fill and the drain.

How long those stretches last depends on the tub and your plumbing. The Ella’s Bubbles Companion holds 65 to 85 gallons with a bather inside, and its fast-fill faucet is rated for up to 16 gallons a minute at 60 psi under ideal home plumbing, according to its spec sheet. At that rate, 60 gallons arrives in under four minutes. At a more typical 7 gallons a minute it takes more than eight. A full walk-in tub typically takes up to about seven minutes to drain, according to a U.S. patent for a faster tub drain, and longer with a slow house drain; our fill and drain time guide shows how to time your own.

That matters more for older bathers. The National Institute on Aging says changes that come with aging can make it harder to notice when your body is getting too cold. It advises keeping the home at at least 68°F and warns that even mildly cool rooms of 60 to 65°F can lead to hypothermia in older adults. It also lists diabetes, thyroid problems, Parkinson’s disease, arthritis and some medicines among the things that make it harder to stay warm. A wet body in a cool bathroom loses heat quickly, which is why “I got cold waiting for it to drain” is one of the most common walk-in tub complaints. The main site lists it among the real walk-in tub problems and how to avoid each.

What does a heated seat and backrest do?

A heated seat and backrest is a set of low-voltage heating pads built into the tub shell behind the seat and the back wall. It warms the surfaces your body touches, so you are not sitting on cool acrylic while the tub fills, and it keeps working after the water has gone.

The Ella owner’s manual describes how its surface heater control works:

  • Switch it on 5 to 10 minutes before you get in. The manual recommends this so the seat is already warm when you sit down.
  • One press starts a 30-minute timer. A red light shows that the heaters are running. Press the button again to turn them off sooner.
  • It reaches a comfortable level within 5 to 10 minutes.
  • It is a small load. The controller plugs into a 120-volt, 15-amp GFCI supply, sends 24-volt DC power to the pads, and lists a 96-watt maximum heater load.

On the Ella Companion and Escape spec sheets, the heated seat and backrest appears under optional features. That is the practical lesson: decide on it when you order, and check that it is written into your quote. Our therapy options builder shows how it fits with jets and the other add-ons.

What does an inline heater do, and what can’t it do?

An inline heater sits in the plumbing of the water-jet system. As the pump circulates bath water through the jets, the heater warms it a little on each pass, which slows the natural cooling of the bath. On the Companion and Escape sheets it is rated at 5.5 amps and 650 watts and is listed with the hydro massage (water-jet) system.

The limit is simple physics. The U.S. Energy Information Administration defines a Btu as the heat needed to raise one pound of water by 1°F, and one kilowatt-hour equals 3,412 Btu. Sixty gallons of water weighs about 500 pounds, at the 8.34 pounds per gallon the U.S. Geological Survey uses. Running a 650-watt heater for a full hour delivers 0.65 kWh, or about 2,218 Btu. Spread over 500 pounds of water, that is a rise of about 4.4°F per hour, and that is the best case with no heat escaping into the air, the tub or you.

650 W × 1 hour = 0.65 kWh 0.65 kWh × 3,412 Btu/kWh = 2,218 Btu 60 gal × 8.34 lb/gal = 500 lb of water 2,218 Btu ÷ 500 lb = about 4.4°F per hour (about 1.1°F per 15 minutes)

So an inline heater is a keep-warm device. It helps a 20-minute soak end close to where it started, but it cannot rescue a lukewarm fill, and it does nothing during the fill or the drain because the jets are not running then. The temperature you fill at is the temperature you get. That makes your home’s hot water supply the real heater; the main site’s water heater sizing guide covers whether yours can keep up.

How warm is too warm?

Heat helps comfort only up to a point. The Ella manual warns that water above 100.4°F (38°C) may be harmful, tells anyone with a heart condition or who takes medicines, herbal remedies or sleep aids to ask a physician first, and warns that staying in a heated bath too long can cause hyperthermia, with dizziness, drowsiness and fainting. The Arthritis Foundation describes 92 to 100°F as a healthy soaking range, and the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission sets 104°F as the most a spa should ever reach.

Three habits keep warmth safe:

  1. Measure, don’t guess. Hands are poor thermometers, especially with diabetes or nerve damage. A floating bath thermometer costs a few dollars. Our arthritis guide has a thermometer check.
  2. Warm the room, not just the water. A heated seat cannot offset a 60°F bathroom. If you need extra heat, the NIA cautions that some portable space heaters are fire or carbon monoxide hazards; keep any heater well away from water, and ask an electrician about a permanent bathroom heater.
  3. Stand slowly. A warm soak can leave you lightheaded. Use the grab bar, and sit a moment after the tub drains before you open the door. Our page on who should skip jets covers blood pressure that drops on standing.

Is a heated seat worth it?

For most bathers who feel the cold, we think it is one of the best-value options, for three reasons. It covers all three phases of the bath, including the drain, where nothing else helps. It costs almost nothing to run: a full 30-minute cycle at the 96-watt maximum uses about 0.05 kWh, under one cent at the July 2026 U.S. average of 18.31 cents per kWh. And it has no jets to clean. The inline heater is worth having if you plan long soaks with the water jets, but it is a partner to the jets, not a substitute for a hot fill or a warm room.

If cost is the deciding factor, put warmth before jet count. A tub with a heated seat, a fast-fill faucet and a good drain often feels better day to day than one with more jets and a slow, chilly drain. The operating cost calculator shows that hot water, not the motors, is the real running cost.

Questions people ask

Does a walk-in tub have a heated seat?

Some do. On Ella’s Bubbles spec sheets the heated seat and backrest is listed as an optional feature, so it is chosen when you order. Ask whether it is in your quote and how long its timer runs.

Can an inline heater heat up a cold bath?

No. It is meant to keep warm water warm. A 650-watt heater can add at most about 4.4°F an hour to 60 gallons, even with no heat escaping, so fill the tub at the temperature you want.

How long does the heated seat stay on?

On the Ella control system described in its owner’s manual, one press starts a 30-minute timer, and pressing the button while it runs turns it off. The manual suggests switching it on 5 to 10 minutes before you get in. If your bath runs longer, press it again once the light goes out.

What water temperature is safe in a walk-in tub?

The Arthritis Foundation describes 92 to 100°F as a healthy soaking range. Ella’s manual warns that water above 100.4°F may be harmful, and the CPSC sets 104°F as the most a spa should ever reach. Use a floating thermometer.

Will a heated seat raise my electric bill?

Barely. Ella’s heater controller has a 96-watt maximum load. A full 30-minute cycle uses about 0.05 kWh, under one cent at the July 2026 U.S. average electricity price.

Can I put a space heater in the bathroom to stay warm?

Be careful. The National Institute on Aging notes that some space heaters are fire hazards or raise carbon monoxide risk. Never put a heater where it could fall into water or get splashed, and ask an electrician about a permanently installed bathroom heater instead.
